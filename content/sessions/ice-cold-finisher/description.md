Any player who finds themselves in front of goal. Strikers, wingers, midfielders arriving late, defenders on set pieces.

If you're better at putting the ball in the net in training than you are on a Saturday, this is the gap this session closes.

## What's actually happening

- Your attention arrives on your own movement. Your finishing is trained to the point where it runs without your conscious attention on it — that's why it comes out so well in training, where you're not thinking about it. Under pressure, that changes. Skilled performers under pressure are measurably more aware of what their own body is doing than they are in practice. And when attention lands on a movement that runs best without it, it comes out with more error in it and more variation. Your conscious mind handles a fraction of what your unconscious handles, and it's slower and less accurate at controlling a trained skill. Same player, same ability, a completely different footballer.
- The reward becomes something you can lose. Past a point, the bigger the prize on offer, the worse a trained movement comes out. That has been measured directly. The moment the chance appears, activity spikes in the ventral striatum — a reward region deep in the brain — in proportion to how big the prize is. Then you have to actually deliver it, and in that same region the activity drops away. By then your brain has already counted the goal as yours, so it isn't something you might gain any more. It's something you might lose. And how much you can't stand losing it is what the drop tracks.

## What we do about it

## The language

In training, you don't score goals. You put the ball in the net, over and over, and that's exactly why it comes out the way it does.

In a game the words change. I could score here. This is my chance. Same action, completely different thing you've just told yourself you're doing — because the moment it becomes a goal, it becomes a prize, and a prize can be won or lost.

This has been tested. When people performing a demanding physical skill were given a simple change to how they described what was riding on it, the reward response to the prize dropped away and they stopped choking. The bigger the drop, the more it protected them. Nothing about the situation changed. Only what they were calling it.

So that's the language throughout this session. The ball goes in the net. Not a goal, not a chance, not a moment.

## The conditions

A skill holds up in the conditions you trained it in — and that includes the state you were in while you trained it. Practise with the pressure present and it holds up when the pressure comes. Practise without it and it doesn't. That's been found in basketball free throws, in darts, in golf putting, and in police officers on live firearms. Same skill, same number of repetitions, two groups. Only the group who had the pressure present held up.

You've had thousands of repetitions of putting the ball in the net calm. Almost none with a game around you.

So we put the game around you. Everything stays — the opposition, the noise, everything that's riding on it. And in the middle of it, you put the ball in the net. Then you go again.

## What this session conditions

- Seeing the picture instantly as it opens up in front of you
- Your attention staying out in the game — the keeper, the space, where it's going
- The finish coming off your foot at the level it comes off in training, automatically, with the whole game around you
- Putting the ball in the net as simply the thing you do — the same action it is in training, whatever is riding on it
- Adapting as it changes — a different angle, a different distance, the keeper moving
- Staying in the play right the way through to the end of it
- Holding all of it when the game raises — bigger occasion, more noise, more on it than ever

## How it works

- Guided visualisation and hypnotherapy get you immersed and fully in the game. Then you go to work.

You're placed in a real game — your last one, or your next one — and you run your own passages of play, over and over. You're guided through it, then handed 45-second intervals to work on your own, without my voice. Then I come back in.

Every time you imagine yourself playing, you engage much of the same motor planning circuitry in your brain as you use to play. So the finishing itself is being trained — real repetitions, without a ball or a pitch.

And they go in with the game around you, which is why they hold up when it's real. Rehearsal that carries the actual setting and the actual feeling of the moment has been shown to produce bigger improvements than rehearsal done calm and neutral.

Two things at once. Your finishing develops, and it gets conditioned to come out in the one place it has never been conditioned to come out.

## How to prepare

- Have your situations ready before you start:

- Your last game. The ones that fell to you — where you were, what was happening, what you could see, who was around you. Take the ones that went in and the ones that didn't. You're running all of them the way you want them.
- Your next game, and where those situations are going to come from.
- Your own clips. Watch them back and find where they actually come from. Most players are surprised by the answer.
- Your coaches. The runs and the positions that get you there. Wyscout if you've got it.
- Do it before you press play, not while the session is running.

Expect this to show up in your numbers. You're not adding situations here. You're putting away more of the ones you already get.

Use it daily. Use it before training. Use it the night before a game.
