## Who this is for

- The player who gets nervous before a game. Sometimes during it.

The heart going in the changing room. The stomach. The legs. The feeling you've spent years wishing wasn't there — because as far as you can tell, it puts you off your game.

It does. But not for the reason you think. And it doesn't have to.

## What's actually happening

- Nerves are your body loading you up. In the hours and minutes before a game your adrenal glands release adrenaline into your blood. It raises your heart rate. It raises the volume of blood your heart pushes out with every beat. It opens your airways wider and puts more glucose into your bloodstream. More oxygen and more fuel are being delivered to your muscles before you've kicked a ball. That feeling in your chest isn't a warning. It's the delivery.

And separately, your brain is being turned up. Deep in your brainstem a small cluster of cells fires noradrenaline right across your cortex. That system sets how alert you are and how strongly your brain responds to what's in front of you. It's not the adrenaline in your blood doing that — the two run in parallel. One loads your body. The other turns your brain up.

The size of that response doesn't decide whether it helps you or hurts you. What you take it to mean does. And that isn't a feeling — it produces real, physical differences inside you.

Read it as a problem, and your blood vessels tighten. Your heart is working just as hard, but it's pushing against narrowed vessels. Less of that oxygenated blood gets delivered. Your body has done all the preparation and you've stopped it from reaching you. At the same time, your attention gets pulled toward the negative — what's uncomfortable, what might go wrong, what people are making of you. All that readiness, pointed inward at yourself instead of out at the game.

Read it as your body getting you ready, and the vessels stay wide open. More blood moves per minute. The fuel and the oxygen get where they were going. And that pull of your attention toward the negative is measurably lower — so it's out there where it belongs, on the game, seeing it, anticipating it.

The players at the very top don't feel it less. Measure elite and non-elite performers and the intensity of what they feel is the same. What separates them is what they take it to mean.

And calm isn't the better option. Take the nerves away and replace them with total relaxation and your body produces nothing. No rise in heart rate. No extra blood moving. Nothing delivered. The energy, the alertness, the readiness — none of it is switched on.

## What this session conditions

- The feeling arriving and telling you that you're ready — automatically, every time
- Welcoming it in the changing room, in the warm up, in the tunnel, wherever it comes for you
- Your attention going straight out onto the game rather than onto how you're feeling
- Taking all of that energy and alertness into your football and playing at your very best level
- Full involvement right the way through the passage of play, to the conclusion
- Holding all of it when the game is bigger and the feeling is stronger — because more of it means more being delivered to you
- This isn't calming down and it isn't pretending you're not nervous. You'll feel exactly what you've always felt. What changes is what it sets off in you.

## How it works

- Guided visualisation and hypnotherapy get you immersed and fully into the moment before a game. Then you go to work.

Here's why this can be conditioned rather than just known. When you imagine the moments before a game, your body produces the real physical response — heart rate rising, more blood being pushed around your body, all of it measured as actually happening during the rehearsal. So you're not sitting there thinking about nerves. You're in them. Every rehearsal attaches the new meaning to the real sensation.

You're guided through it, then handed 45-second intervals to run it yourself, over and over, without my voice. Back to the feeling, forward into your football, then again. Then I come back in.

Knowing something is nice. Conditioning it, so it's wired in and it's simply how you respond, is where the improvement comes from.

## How to prepare

- Have this ready before you start:

- Where you feel it. The changing room, the warm up, the tunnel, the pitch before kick off. Whichever moment is the one for you.
- Where it lives in your body. Everybody is different. Chest, stomach, legs, hands, breathing. Be specific about yours — that's the sensation the conditioning attaches to.
- What you look like in the game when you're thriving. You're moving forward from that feeling straight into your football, so know the level you're rehearsing at.
- Do this before you start the session, not during it.

A note on timing. This is your match preparation — the hours and minutes before you play. It isn't for the night before or the morning of a game. In those situations you do want to be relaxed, with your focus off the game entirely.

Use it daily. Use it before training. Use it in the build-up to a game.
